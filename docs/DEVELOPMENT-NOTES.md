# Ireland's Railways: Then and Now

A map built with the ArcGIS Maps SDK for JavaScript (4.30) that compares Ireland's railway network in any year from 1834 to today. Move the slider and lines are coloured as:

- **Red:** still in use today
- **Blue:** open in that year, closed since
- **Sepia, dashed:** already closed by that year
- **Sepia, dotted:** closed, but no closure date is recorded

The look follows the 1920 Railway Clearing House map of Ireland: lines in its engraver's red and blue on cream paper (a tint over Esri's grey basemap), a pale green sea drawn as the old map draws it (an ink coastline about a pixel and a half wide, a deeper green inshore fading out to sea, and fine engraved water lines following the coast, all from a distance-to-coast field in `sea3d.js`; `?sea=living` swaps in the moving, sunlit sea, `?sea=flat` the flat fill), the main loughs in the same green with an ink edge and the main rivers as fine lines (`water.js`, from Natural Earth via `node scripts/fetch-water.mjs`), the hills and mountains in light hachures (short strokes down each slope, closer and heavier where it's steeper, traced from open terrain data by `node scripts/make-hachures.mjs` into `data/hachures.json`; `hachures.js` draws them with the principal peaks named in italic), place names set as on the 1920 sheet (towns on the railway in bold grotesque capitals, each with a small ink-ringed station circle on its line that shows only while the line is open; places off the railway in small italic), revealed by rank as you zoom in (the cities and the principal towns of 1920, picked by hand rather than by today's population, at the home view; then the other large towns, towns, rail villages and the rest) and kept clear of each other, with the more important name winning (`places.js`, from `data/towns.json`; the panel's "Name smaller towns and villages" switch leaves only the cities and principal towns); names are in English as on the 1920 sheet (the data's Irish-only Gaeltacht names are given the English names the map used, e.g. Dungloe, Belmullet, Dingle), and the towns that lost their railway carry no second name on their columns, since the place names already give it; the seas named in widely spaced italic capitals (Atlantic Ocean, Irish Sea, North Channel, St. George's Channel, Celtic Sea), and the bays and loughs in small italic as you zoom in (`seas.js`); lines drawn as on the sheet, their colour edged in a darker tone of itself; and county names in widely spaced roman capitals, set well clear of the county town of the same name (and, where that town is named at the home view, held back until you zoom in, so the two never read as one name printed twice) (hidden on narrow screens, where they'd crowd the island). Peak and sea names are labels like the towns', so where two would overlap one gives way rather than printing over the other; the peaks are named only closer in. County names run faintly behind everything, as on the old sheet, and never knock a town's name off. The cream land is fully opaque, so the grey basemap's own printed names don't show through as faint duplicates. Towns cut off are plum. The ground that lost its railway is scorched like burnt paper (`lostland.js`, switch "Scorch the ground that lost its railway"): land once within 10 km of a line open at some point up to the chosen year, with none open within 10 km that year, is browned, with a charred rim that crumbles in by a noise pattern over 5 km (lines with no recorded closure count as open until the present day). It burns as the railways vanish, a bit at a time: as a closing line pulls back along its length, the ground beside it catches just as its retreating tip passes, and the fire spreads out from the old track with emitted ember light (a glowing rim, flickering sparks, a warm halo) and faint smoke rising off it, then smoulders and cools to char. It moves slowly on purpose (about 2.6 s to spread, 5 s to cool), so the eye can follow it; the smoke is worked out in its own pass at a quarter of the pixels, so the fire costs the frame rate little. Going back in years, the ground recovers as a line regrows. When the map lands it burns in the same way, the closures in the order they happened. Drawing it is our own WebGL pass over the finished ground, which finds each pixel's point on the ground from the depth buffer and multiplies the scorch in, so the engraved ink stays dark through it; the maths (two distance fields on a 1 km grid, and each cell's burn time from the nearest point of a moving line) runs in a worker (`lostfield.js`, `lostland-worker.js`), so a change of year doesn't stall the page. (`window.__burnAt = ms` in the console freezes a burn to inspect it.)

The animations move like cut paper on an animation stand (`paper.js`): the swans, the lines, the towns and the scorch step to a new drawing 12 times a second and hold in between, while the camera still moves smoothly. Between steps nothing animated needs redrawing, so at rest the scene draws about 12 times a second rather than every frame. The railway lines are drawn with our own WebGL (`lines3d.js`), not as ArcGIS graphics: profiling playback showed that every change to a draped graphic, however small, made ArcGIS redraw the draped overlay (about 65 ms a time), holding playback to about 12 fps with 600-800 ms of every second spent in long tasks. Now all 630 lines are uploaded once as short segments, each drawn as a screen-space capsule (cased in a darker tone of their colour, round caps and joins, trackbeds dashed or dotted), lifted 60 m over the ground sampled from the terrain and depth-tested; how much of each line shows lives in a small texture, so animating costs a few kilobytes of upload. Playback now runs at 57-59 fps with no long tasks. Each line keeps an invisible, never-changing ArcGIS copy so a click still opens its details. The towns' columns move by copies of their own points, and each step of them is held to 6 ms of the page's time, any rest finished over the next frame or two at the same beat. Every map colour is at least 3:1 against the land, and red and blue stay far apart for colour-blind readers; the closed lines also keep their dashes and dots.

The interface takes the same period: the opening title is a cartouche after the old map's title panel ("IRELAND" in the old title's own engraved lettering, lifted from a photo of the 1920 map (public domain) by `scripts/trace-title.py`, which evens out the paper's lighting, takes the fold crease's shading out of the E and smart-fills the columns it ruined (a fill that spreads mostly sideways from the engraving either side, as the strokes crossing the fold run that way, then sharpened back to ink or paper), keeps the ink, then undoes the photo's angle letter by letter from its longest straight lines (cap line, foot line and upright, fitted with outliers dropped): rotation, lean and keystone out, every letter scaled to one cap height on one baseline, and spaced optically (the same average white between each pair) with engraver's small caps around it) on the linen-mounted, folded map's green; the panel and drawers are paper sheets with the same scooped corners and a fine grain; and the panel's figures are buff Edmondson railway tickets. Type: Roboto Slab 800 (display), IM Fell English SC (small caps), Libre Caslon Text (text) and Archivo (labels and controls), from Google Fonts.

Lines that hadn't opened yet in the chosen year are hidden. Click a line for its dates, gauge, and a link to the source.

The page opens on a title card while the map loads, then lifts on a view pulled back from Ireland, with Britain still on the map and a 3D sky of cirrus over both (`clouds3d.js`), modelled on how it looks and moves over Ireland: a long jet-stream band crossing the island from south-west to north-east, with looser patches on three levels, all drifting together with the Atlantic jet (higher layers faster) and thinning away as the camera drops. Mares' tails hook the same way, and each streak casts a faint shadow. The heights are about two to three times real cirrus (6-13 km), so the layers still slide past each other a little. The hills of both islands are hachured from the start too: the hachures are drawn with our own WebGL (`hachures.js`), so they cost the flight nothing (as a draped layer they had to be redrawn into the ground for every tile while the camera zoomed, so they used to wait until it landed), and lie on the terrain once it loads. Britain's come from `node scripts/make-hachures.mjs britain` (`data/hachures-britain.json`, a little sparser) and fade out with Britain. The county names are there from the start as well, each placed clear of the town names and station circles (worked out against both at the home view and zoomed in, moving a name at most 14 km so it stays in its county). Britain's shires can be lettered too (`createShireNames` in `counties.js`, `SHOW_SHIRES` in `app.js`), but are off for now. The swans fly over the names: ArcGIS draws its text after every stage a render node can use, so their bodies are drawn on a transparent canvas laid over the map with the same camera, while their shadows stay on the ground under the words. Both coastlines, Ireland's and Britain's, are inked from the start of the flight (`coastink.js`, our own WebGL, since outlines on the draped land made the flight hitch). Nothing fades during the flight: once the camera has stopped, one 1.8 s fade sinks Britain (and its ink) under the sea while the railway lines and the panel fade in together, and Ireland's ink then hands over to the sea's own inked coast. The railway lines start hidden. As the camera zooms in, the camera drops through the clouds about 5 s into the 8 s flight, once the title card has gone; Britain sinks under the sea after it lands (`intro.js`; click to skip once it's flying). Both coastlines come from Esri's detailed World Countries layer, saved to `data/ireland.geojson` (to about 50 m, so the land and sea follow the basemap's coast into the bays) and `data/britain.geojson` (looser) by `scripts/fetch-data.mjs`; if Britain's is missing and the live request fails, the opening starts with Britain already covered. Each strand is a card stretched along the sky, filled with soft fibres and feathered at the edges; the jet band has a crisper north-western edge and faint, irregular billows across it. They're drawn with our own WebGL inside the scene (an ArcGIS `RenderNode`), sorted back to front in one draw call, so they move in step with the camera every frame. They're drawn at half resolution (at most 1.2 million pixels) and laid over the scene, since the fibre shader is costly: on an integrated GPU at full resolution a frame took about 2 s. Behind the title card the opening times a few frames with the clouds up; if they're slow (over 50 ms), or if three frames in a row during the flight take over 100 ms, it flies without them (the console says so). Terrain (world elevation) is only added once the opening lands, and the home view is framed with computed cameras, so the flight starts within seconds rather than waiting on the elevation service. Add `?debug&perf` to time the flight's frames (summary in the console), with `clouds=off|still|nofade`, `britain=still` and `sea=flat` to switch parts off and compare. The sea is a flat fill during the flight; the living sea fades in after landing.

Once the flight's start and landing are known, the cloud patches are strung along the camera's own path instead (`fitPath` in `clouds3d.js`): each sits part-way along it, at about the camera's height as it passes, some dead ahead and some to the sides, so the camera flies down through the cloud rather than over it. Each strand shows one of eight cirrus drawings made once at load in a worker (`cloud-drawings.js`): a slim wisp tapering to fine tails, with fine broken streaks along it and a crisp outline in a cool grey-blue (lighter than the map's ink, so it never reads as a coast), drawn at full resolution so the outline stays steady. On the path they're smaller and fuller than the high cirrus, cast no shadows, thin away as the camera reaches each one (so the magnified outline never smears across the view) and fade if seen end-on; the jet band is left out. The volumetric clouds (`?vol`, `?vol=cirrus`) take an outline too, inked in their final pass where the cloud's cover crosses a threshold. The scene runs on ArcGIS's "high" quality profile: on the default "medium" it switched between full and half resolution from frame to frame, so the inked coast, the lines and the text halos swelled and thinned. The sea draws without testing against the terrain's depth: tested against it, coastal hills poked through as finer terrain loaded tile by tile, and the inked coastline blinked. The swans draw after the sea, so it can't cover them.

The opening now uses the volumetric cirrus by default, outlined as on old maps, and moved onto the flight path (`fitPath` in `volclouds.js`): the thin sheet sits at the camera's height halfway down, centred under it there, so the camera drops through the streaks mid-flight; at that height it casts no shadows. With `?debug` a box at the bottom left lists what the opening did and why (also logged to the console as `[opening]` warnings): whether it flew, and with or without clouds; add `?clouds=drawn` for the drawn cirrus cards on the flight path described above, or `?vol` for experimental volumetric cumulus. The volumetric cirrus (`volclouds.js`): soft, fibrous streaks 13-15.5 km up, drawn out along the jet with clear sky between, densest near its axis and in scattered patches away from it, modelled on NASA's VIIRS image of jet cirrus. Each streak is a soft body of stretched noise on a turbulent flow, with denser heads and translucent trails; fine fibres (crests of noise stretched ~40x along the flow) texture it and fray its edges. The cirrus is a thin sheet, so it's sampled at three heights at full resolution instead of marched, with every pattern filtered to the pixel's size so it doesn't alias; it runs at full frame rate. As it clears, the streaks thin to wisps before they go. Each time the map opens the cirrus starts from a slightly different point in its pattern (within 400 km along the jet and 150 km across), so the streaks differ but the jet stays over Ireland; with `?debug`, `window.__volSeed(along, across)` sets it. Its look is set by named constants in the cirrus preset; with `?debug`, `window.__volHot()` reloads them without reloading the map. The cumulus is ray marched the way game engines draw clouds: each pixel's ray crosses a shell of sky 5-11 km up, sampling a tiling 3D noise volume (`cloud-noise.js`, built in a worker) for density, with a few samples towards the sun for light (Beer's law plus a softer multiple-scattering term), and a short march from the ground for the clouds' shadows. It's drawn at 40% resolution and laid over the scene. During the flight the heaps shrink and break up, rather than fading. It's heavier on the GPU than the cirrus (roughly half the frame rate in a first measurement).

The sea is our own water shader (`sea3d.js`): irregular Atlantic swell (Gerstner-style wave trains with bent crests and wave sets), sky reflection that grows towards the horizon, a sun sheen, pale shallows inshore and surf rolling onto the coast, from a distance-to-coast field worked out at start-up. The moving surface reaches far beyond anything the map shows and fades into the flat sea over its outer 30%, so it has no visible edge. At full HD with 2x pixel density it costs about 3 fps (57 to 54). With `?debug`, `window.__seaFrame()` returns a PNG of the live frame (use it rather than `view.takeScreenshot`, which draws custom render nodes with a different camera). The map is a 3D scene with real terrain; the opening starts tilted and turned and straightens as it comes down, landing looking straight down with north up, the whole island beside the panel. Drag to pan, right-drag to rotate or change the tilt, and scroll to zoom.

Heather columns are towns and villages that had lost their railway by the chosen year. Taller columns mean more people today. Columns rise out of the ground as each town loses its line. Railway lines draw themselves along their length when they open and pull back when they close, leaving a faint dashed trackbed.

The **Story** button walks through seven chapters, each of which sets up the map. It's told through one town, Navan, the largest on the island without a station, which has a short note in every chapter (its two lines of the 1850s–60s, the passenger closures of 1947 and 1958, the Tara Mines freight still running past it, the M3, the 2010 reopening to M3 Parkway, and the new line in the 2050 plan). Each chapter leads with one headline number; the rest of its charts and figures are folded under "Show the numbers".

1. Growth, 1834–1920: the map plays the network spreading.
2. Why they fell: partition, the Civil War, mergers, the road, the Emergency, losses and the Beddy and Benson reports, and a population at its lowest in 1961.
3. The fall, 1950–1975: the map plays the closures; 63% of the 1920 railway is gone.
4. Travel, "Not slower, but no choice": the last trains' timetables (Derry–Dublin and Derry–Belfast 1958, Enniskillen–Belfast 1957, Tralee–Dingle in the 1930s) against today's fastest bus and a car without traffic. The old trains were no faster than today's express buses, so the chapter says what was really lost was the choice, and that car growth happened all over Europe. Only routes with a sourced old timetable are shown; West Clare is left out, as its times couldn't be verified.
5. The places hit: the north-west and border counties with no railway, and the people in the towns cut off.
6. If they'd stayed: one estimate (journeys a year), what it would mean for people, the economy and the air, and the "To be fair" note beside it.
7. 2050: the All-Island Strategic Rail Review's network, with the page in its future look (`future.js`) and the 1920 railway drawn faintly beneath it in amber, so it's plain what would be put back and what wouldn't. Not yet adopted as policy.

The first, longer version of the story (nine chapters, more numbers on show) is kept as `story-classic.js`: open the map with `?story=classic` to use it.

Charts are small multiples on one time axis, with hover and a table view. Figures and sources are in `story.js`. The rail and towns series come from the map data (`data/rail-series.json`).

The 3D landmark models are switched off for now (`SHOW_LANDMARKS` in `app.js`). When on, landmarks mark the main cities, famous historic sites (Newgrange, the Rock of Cashel, the Giant's Causeway and others) and railway heritage spots (Dún Laoghaire, the Listowel monorail, Moyasta, Stranorlar, Ballydehob). Click one for a short note. The list is in `landmarks.js`.

Four 3D swans in a V, the Children of Lir, fly across the island from the upper left of the opening view to the lower right, then come round again. They can be turned off in the panel, and clicking one tells their story. They're drawn with our own WebGL (`swans.js`): one low-poly mesh, with the wings bent by the vertex shader each frame, so the beat is smooth and costs ArcGIS nothing.

Add `?debug` to the URL to expose the animation objects as `window.__anim` for profiling, and `window.__introClouds(t)` to pose the opening clouds at any point of the flight (0–1). `?debug&hold` holds the opening until you call `window.__introGo()`. The panel's "If the closed lines ran today" section gives rough estimates of journeys and car trips saved. See [analysis/FINDINGS.md](analysis/FINDINGS.md) for the method and sources. To rebuild the towns layer:

```bash
node analysis/classify-towns.mjs data/places.json data/rail.geojson data/towns.json
```

```bash
node analysis/estimate.mjs
```

A town's closure year is when the last closed line within 3 km of it closed (the year it lost its railway), not the nearest one: Letterkenny's nearest was the Burtonport extension (1947), but its lines to Derry and Strabane ran until 1953 and 1960. The line data records when a line closed altogether, so a few towns show the year freight stopped rather than passengers. `data/rail-series.json` (towns cut off by year, for the story) is counted from the same years.

## Run it

```bash
npm run fetch-data
```

```bash
npm run serve
```

Then open http://localhost:8765. The server (`scripts/serve.mjs`) sends every file with `Cache-Control: no-store`, so a normal refresh always picks up changes. Other static servers let the browser keep old copies, and one stale module stops the whole app from starting.

`fetch-data` saves the whole island's railways to `data/rail.geojson`. If that file is missing, the app asks the Overpass API directly when it loads, which is slower.

The page has to be served over HTTP. Opening `index.html` from disk won't load the local data file.

## Data notes

- Railway data comes from [OpenHistoricalMap](https://www.openhistoricalmap.org) (CC0). Unlike OpenStreetMap, which only maps what survives on the ground today, OpenHistoricalMap keeps closed lines complete and dates them with `start_date` and `end_date`.
- A line counts as still in use when it has no `end_date`. If a closed line is missing its `end_date` in OpenHistoricalMap, it will wrongly show as in use.
- Gauge comes from the `gauge` tag. Ireland's 3 ft narrow-gauge lines are tagged `railway=rail` with `gauge=914`.
- Trams and stations are left out.
- Few ways have names, so most popups show "Unnamed line".
- Basemap: Esri World Light Gray. To use Esri's vector basemap instead, put an ArcGIS API key in `config.js`.

Every section of line is named by the places at its ends, as a ticket names a journey ("Mallow – Cork"): OpenHistoricalMap names only 32 of the 630 sections, some of them only a bridge, so `node scripts/name-lines.mjs` takes the best town near each end (one with a station first, then the larger, then the nearer, in the English form the map uses) and saves `data/line-names.json`; a real line name in the data ("Tralee and Dingle Light Railway") is kept too. A line's details open as a railway card ticket of the time, and only the ticket: no ArcGIS popup frame (its popup is built from components the page's CSS can't restyle), but our own (`ticketpop.js`) pinned to the point clicked by a little tail, following it as the map moves, closed by a punched hole in its corner, on Escape or by clicking the map elsewhere, with "Zoom to" on the ticket itself. The ticket: buff card with a perforated edge, the company across the top ("Irish Railways" where the data has none), FROM and TO in heavy capitals, a band in the line's map colour saying how it stands, the opening date, length and gauge, and a red serial number (from the OpenHistoricalMap way). The county names come in with the panel and the railway lines once the opening lands.

The towns that lost their railway open as tickets too, to a place the railway no longer goes: the town's name, a plum band with the year the trains stopped, and the town now (people, distance to a line in use, area). The swans open a card of their own: the legend of the Children of Lir as a leaf of an old Irish manuscript by night, deep blue vellum with a slow silver shimmer, a gold border, the title in an uncial hand (Uncial Antiqua), the three waters and their 300 years each, and white feathers drifting down it (still, for readers who ask for reduced motion). Only one card is open at a time.

The panel speaks the 1920 sheet's language too, with no modern controls: the key is the sheet's "Reference" box (a double rule on the paper, its lines drawn cased as on the map, the towns that lost their railway as a little plum column), the towns cut off sit on a plum ticket stub, the switches are inked tick boxes, the year slider a double rule inked red up to the year with an ink lozenge to drag, the play button an ink seal, and the Story and Findings buttons letterpress, in the map's small caps.

The scorch only ever lies on the land: a mask of the island at 0.2 km a pixel (its outline filled, the loughs cut out) keeps it, its rim, embers and smoke off the sea and the loughs. The city on the Foyle is named Derry throughout (the city, its county, and the lines that run there).
